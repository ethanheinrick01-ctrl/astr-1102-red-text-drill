More fuel, much faster use
The lecture calls stars above about eight solar masses massive. They are uncommon compared with lower-mass stars. Greater core pressure and temperature allow faster nuclear reactions. Although a massive star starts with more fuel, its much greater luminosity uses that fuel faster, giving it a shorter life.

Winds build a surrounding environment
Strong radiation can drive material away from a massive star. Over its lifetime, winds and outbursts leave gas around it. When the star eventually explodes, the explosion can run into that previously expelled material. This explains the lecture’s dirty environment: the surroundings carry a history of the star’s own mass loss.

Mass loss can expose deeper layers
If enough hydrogen-rich envelope is removed, hotter, helium-rich layers become exposed. This can produce a Wolf Rayet star. The star has changed what we can see at its surface; the lost material remains outside it. Do not identify every glowing shell as a planetary nebula. The progenitor and the physical process matter.
