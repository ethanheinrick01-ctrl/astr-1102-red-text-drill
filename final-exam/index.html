Our Galaxy

Lecture 31 · Dr. Chatzopoulos

Lecture slides
Magenta · terms and names Lime · meaning and sequence Yellow highlight · lecture emphasis Peach + label · understanding checkpoint

The first maps of the Milky Way

The Milky Way is our galaxy, with a vast disk of stars that includes our solar system. Galileo presented evidence that Earth orbits the Sun and was put under house arrest in 1633. Astronomers still put the Sun near the center of the Galaxy until the 1920s.

  • William Herschel made the first attempt to map the Galaxy by counting stars. He saw roughly the same star density in different directions along the plane and placed us at the center. He also discovered Uranus and the motion of binary stars.
  • Caroline Herschel worked with William and then made discoveries independently, including eight comets and fourteen nebulae.
  • Jacobus Kapteyn used stellar brightness and motion data from 40 observatories and discovered that the Galaxy rotates. His model gave the Galaxy a diameter of 17,000 parsecs.
    Understanding checkpoint

    Kapteyn's map placed the Sun at the center. The highlighted caption identifies that as a starting point of his map: sun (assumed to be at the center of the system).

Worked example

Suppose you count about the same number of visible stars in each direction along the Milky Way. That puts you near the middle of the stars you can see. Measuring how far you can see in each direction tells you whether you have mapped the whole Galaxy.

Interstellar dust

  • Interstellar dust scatters light from distant stars. Most of it lies in the Galactic plane. Foreground clouds make dark patches by obscuring the stars behind them.
    Understanding checkpoint

    Dust limited early star maps to our local neighborhood. Counting the stars visible through that dust made the Sun appear to be at the center.

  • Robert Trumpler found evidence for interstellar dust by studying open clusters. He used cluster size to estimate distance geometrically and found that clusters were fainter than expected at those distances. He published the result in 1930.
  • Kapteyn had considered absorption as early as 1909. Shapley showed in 1915 that the lines of sight to distant globular clusters contained little dust.

Worked example

A cluster looks too faint for its distance estimated from size. Dust along the line of sight reduces the starlight reaching us, explaining the difference.

RR Lyrae and globular clusters

  • Globular clusters can be seen above the dusty disk, like the tops of tall buildings seen above fog.
  • RR Lyrae stars are pulsating evolved stars with about the same luminosity. They're less luminous than Cepheids, more common, and plentiful in globular clusters.
    Understanding checkpoint

    Compare an RR Lyrae star's apparent brightness with its known luminosity to find its distance. That gives you the distance to the cluster containing it.

  • Harlow Shapley combined distances and sky positions for 93 globular clusters to map their three-dimensional distribution in the 1920s. Some were about 100,000 light-years away. The clusters formed a sphere centered toward Sagittarius, away from Earth.

Worked example

Two RR Lyrae stars in different globular clusters have about the same luminosity. The star that appears fainter is in the farther cluster. Use its sky position and distance to place the cluster on the three-dimensional map.

Our place in the Milky Way

  • The Sun is in the disk 8 kpc (8,000 pc, or about 26,000 light-years) from the Galactic center. We look toward Sagittarius to see the central region.
  • The disk is the flat part of the Galaxy, with a bulge in the middle. At the center is a dense nucleus containing a black hole.
  • The halo is the spherical region surrounding the disk. It contains globular clusters.
    Understanding checkpoint

    On the side-view figure, find the Sun in the disk, the nucleus in the bulge, and globular clusters above and below the disk.

Worked example

Draw a line from the Sun to the nucleus on the side view. The line is about 8 kpc long. Now look at the face-on model to see that the Sun is partway out in the disk.

The infrared Milky Way

Dust scattering affects blue light more strongly than red light. Longer wavelengths let astronomers look through more dust.

  • Near-infrared images reveal the stars through the dust, including the bright concentration of stars toward the bulge.
  • Far-infrared images show emission from warm dust.
    Understanding checkpoint

    Use near-infrared to see the stars and far-infrared to see where warm dust is glowing.

Worked example

The lower panel of the infrared slide shows a bright central bulge. It's the near-infrared view, where light from stars traces the shape of the Galaxy.

  • Herschel and Kapteyn mapped visible stars and placed the Sun at the center.
  • Trumpler used open clusters to show that dust dims starlight.
  • Shapley used RR Lyrae distances to map globular clusters around Sagittarius.
  • The Sun is 8 kpc from the Galactic center.
  • Near-infrared reveals stars. Far-infrared reveals warm dust.

Stellar populations

The Sun lies in the disk 8 kpc, or about 26,000 light-years, from the Galactic center. Dust in the plane hides distant stars in visible light. Shapley located the center toward Sagittarius by mapping globular clusters. Review Lecture 31.

  • Population I stars are metal-rich and typically younger. They populate the disk and some of the bulge. The slide shows them in blue.
  • Population II stars are old and metal-poor. They occur in globular clusters, among isolated high-velocity stars in the halo, and in some of the bulge. The slide shows them in red.
  • The bulge, shown in yellow, contains a mix of young and old stars.
    Understanding checkpoint

    Associate the disk with younger Population I stars and the halo’s globular clusters with old Population II stars. The bulge contains both populations.

Worked example

A Type II supernova comes from the collapse of a massive star. The disk is the best place to look because it contains young, massive stars.

See the population diagram and the Type II class vote

Mapping hydrogen at 21 cm

Radio waves let us see through the dust that obscures the Galactic plane. Cold interstellar hydrogen produces no visible hydrogen emission. Optical hydrogen lines come from hotter gas, such as gas in an emission nebula.

  • Cold neutral hydrogen has two closely spaced lowest energy states. A spin flip from the higher state to the lower one emits a radio wave with a wavelength of 21 cm. Hendrik van de Hulst predicted the line in 1944. It was first detected in 1951.
  • 21 cm maps show gas clouds. Far-infrared maps show warm dust clouds. Both maps show a strong concentration in the plane.
  • The Doppler shift of the line gives the gas’s speed toward or away from us. With the Galaxy’s rotation, astronomers can use that speed to estimate the cloud’s distance.
    Understanding checkpoint

    The near–far ambiguity occurs when the same measured speed fits two distances along one line of sight.

Worked example

A cloud’s 21 cm line arrives at a shorter wavelength than it emits at rest. That blueshift means the cloud is approaching us along our line of sight. A redshift means the cloud is receding.

Evidence for spiral arms

Neutral-hydrogen radio maps show spiral patterns made of broken, uneven arcs. Young, hot stars are concentrated in the nearby arms. Giant molecular clouds trace the arms too. Both support the picture of spiral arms as regions of active star formation.

  • The lecture’s map shows two major arms, Perseus and Scutum-Centaurus. Sagittarius, Norma, and the Outer Arm are smaller arms.
  • The Sun is in the Orion Spur, the local branch marked near the Sun on the map.
Understanding checkpoint

Use radio maps to trace cold gas. Young stars and molecular clouds give additional evidence of where the arms are.

Worked example

Compare the three views of M83. Radio shows cold gas and complex arms, visible light picks out young hot stars and active star formation, and infrared reveals a smoother distribution of stars, including between the arms.

A Galactic year

  • Earth’s orbit carries us around the Sun at about 30 km/s. The Sun’s orbit carries us around the Galaxy at about 220 km/s. To find the Sun’s speed, astronomers measure its motion relative to globular clusters and other galaxies, which they take to be stationary on average.
  • A Galactic year is one trip by the Sun around the center of the Milky Way. The Sun takes about 220 million years per trip and is about 23 Galactic years old.
    Understanding checkpoint

    Use the Sun’s orbital speed of 220 km/s and its 8 kpc distance from the Galactic center to find the Galactic year.

Worked example

Orbital period is circumference divided by speed: P = 2πR / v. Using R ≈ 2.5 × 1020 m and v = 2.2 × 105 m/s gives about 7.1 × 1015 seconds, or 220 million years.

Rotation curves and dark matter

A rotation curve shows orbital speed at different distances from the Galactic center. Astronomers use Doppler shifts of neutral hydrogen’s 21 cm line to measure rotation.

  • In solid-body rotation, outer points move faster, as on a spinning disc. In Keplerian rotation, inner orbits move faster, as planets do around the Sun. Well out in the Milky Way’s disk, orbital speed stays roughly level.
  • Orbital speed reveals how much mass is inside an orbit. The Sun’s speed requires about 9 × 1010 solar masses inside its orbit. The Galaxy could contain 1012 solar masses in total. The rotation curve indicates that more than 90% of the mass lies farther out than the Sun.
  • Dark matter helps explain the high speeds seen far out in galaxy rotation curves. A dark nebula consists of ordinary gas and dust that obscure visible light.
    Understanding checkpoint

    High speeds far from the center require substantial mass at large distances from the center.

Worked example

On the lecture’s graph, the measured blue curve stays high toward the outside. The dashed Keplerian curve falls. The difference shows why a model with mass concentrated at the center predicts speeds that are too low in the outer disk.

Orbits at the Galactic center

The Galactic nucleus is the dense central region containing a black hole. On the lecture’s orbit plot, astronomers track nearby stars over time. Their paths loop around the same invisible center.

Understanding checkpoint

Small stellar orbits show that mass is packed into the Galactic center. The rotation curve traces mass across the much larger galaxy.

Follow the colored measurements for a star on the orbit plot. Each dot is a position. The shared center of the orbits marks the location of the central black hole, whose gravity bends the stars’ paths around it.

  • Cold neutral hydrogen emits at 21 cm. Young stars and molecular clouds map spiral arms.
  • The Sun is in the Orion Spur. It moves around the Galaxy at 220 km/s and takes 220 million years per orbit.

The Galactic Center and Galaxy Types

Lecture 33 · Dr. Chatzopoulos

Lecture slides
Magenta · terms and names Lime · meaning and sequence Yellow highlight · lecture emphasis Peach + label · understanding checkpoint

From the rotation curve to the nucleus

The Galaxy’s rotation curve reveals the mass inside stellar orbits. The Sun’s speed of 220 km/s requires about 9 × 1010 solar masses inside its orbit. High speeds in the outer disk require substantial mass far from the center; the total Galactic mass could be 1012 solar masses.

The two major arms are Perseus and Scutum-Centaurus. Sagittarius, Norma and the Outer Arm are smaller structures; the Sun lies in the Orion Spur. Infrared observations reveal the central region through dust. A wide far-infrared view shows the dusty plane, while a near-infrared close-up shows a dense stellar cluster.

Sagittarius A is a strong radio source at the Galactic center. Fast electrons moving in a magnetic field produce synchrotron radiation. The bright central knot is Sagittarius A*, or Sgr A*. Radio images also show gas rotating around the central region.

Understanding checkpoint

The rotation curve measures mass across the Galaxy. The tight stellar orbits at the nucleus reveal a compact central mass.

Worked example

Compare the wide infrared panel with its close-up. The bright central patch resolves into many stars when the view narrows.

Weighing Sagittarius A*

Sagittarius A* is the Milky Way’s central supermassive black hole. It lies at the dynamical center around which nearby stars orbit. Their high speeds require about four million solar masses concentrated there. Its radio brightness, lack of expansion and faint infrared appearance distinguish it from a normal star, pulsar or expanding supernova remnant.

Astronomers combine astrometry measurements of positions and motion on the sky with radial velocities measured from Doppler shifts. Applying orbital methods gives a black-hole mass of (4.1 ± 0.6) × 106 solar masses and a Galactic-center distance of 8000 ± 600 pc.

S0-2 is an O8 or B0 main-sequence star of about 15–20 solar masses with an orbital period of about 15 years. The slide also gives S0-16’s closest approach as about 45 AU, with a speed of 12,000 km/s. The Galactic-center work received the 2020 Nobel Prize; the EHT imaged Sgr A* in 2022.

Understanding checkpoint

The black-hole mass comes from nearby stellar orbits. Do not substitute the rotation speeds of the entire Galactic disk.

Worked example

On the orbit figure, trace a colored stellar path around the common center. The path and speed reveal how much central mass is needed to hold that star in its orbit.

Tidal stretching and galaxy shapes

Stellar spaghettification is the stretching of a star by tidal gravity near a black hole. The illustration shows material drawn from the star toward the black hole.

Spiral galaxies have disks with spiral arms. Barred spirals have a central bar with arms extending from it. Elliptical galaxies range from giant systems larger than the Milky Way to dwarf systems with masses comparable to globular clusters. Irregular galaxies do not fit the regular spiral or elliptical shapes. The Large and Small Magellanic Clouds are irregular satellites of our Galaxy.

In the Hubble tuning fork galaxies are arranged by shape, E0 appears round and E7 strongly elongated. The spiral branches distinguish normal spirals (S) from barred spirals (SB). Along each branch, a, b and c distinguish the displayed bulge and arm patterns; the c examples have more open arms.

Understanding checkpoint

The tuning fork is a classification diagram, not a left-to-right evolutionary sequence.

Worked example

Compare the normal spiral and barred-spiral photographs. Look through the center for a bar before comparing the arm pattern. Compare an elongated elliptical with a round one to distinguish E7 from E0.

Rotation, gas and stellar populations

Spiral and barred spiral galaxies rotate and have flattened disks. Their stars orbit the center. Gas and dust support ongoing star formation. Their populations are mixed: older Population II stars occupy the halo and bulge, while young Population I stars occupy the disk.

Elliptical galaxies have mostly random stellar motions and little rotation. Many stars pass near the center. They usually have little gas or dust, no active star formation and mostly old Population II stars. The lecture associates them with Type Ia supernovae rather than Type II.

An elliptical cannot turn into a spiral along Hubble’s proposed sequence: it lacks the required rotation, and much of its gas is locked in low-mass stars. The tuning fork describes differences in rotation and structure, not successive ages.

Understanding checkpoint

A galaxy’s shape connects to its stellar motions, gas supply and star formation.

Worked example

A dusty rotating disk with young stars fits a spiral. A system dominated by old stars, random motions and little gas fits an elliptical.

Interactions, starbursts and rings

Galaxies interact gravitationally. Hydrogen maps can show connections between galaxies that look separate in visible light. Direct collisions can reshape and merge galaxies and may help create spiral arms.

During an encounter, tidal compression squeezes gas and triggers star formation. A burst of widespread star formation is a starburst episode. When a small galaxy passes through the middle of a larger one, ripples of star formation can produce a ring galaxy. Hoag’s object is the lecture’s ring-galaxy example.

Understanding checkpoint

The gas compression connects the interaction to the new stars.

Worked example

Compare the visible and hydrogen views of M81, M82 and NGC 3077. The hydrogen view reveals extended gas structures linking the systems.

Keep these straight

  • Galactic rotation curve → mass distribution; central stellar orbits → compact central mass.
  • Sagittarius A* contains about four million solar masses.
  • Spirals rotate and contain gas, dust and mixed stellar populations.
  • Ellipticals have mostly random motions and old stars, with little gas and no active star formation.
  • Tidal compression can trigger starbursts; a central passage can produce a ring of new stars.

Galaxy Distances and the Expanding Universe

Lecture 34 · Dr. Chatzopoulos

Lecture slides
Magenta · terms and names Lime · meaning and sequence Yellow highlight · lecture emphasis Peach + label · understanding checkpoint

Galaxy types and distance methods

Spirals have rotating, gas-rich disks with young stars and older halo and bulge populations. Ellipticals have little rotation, mostly old stars, little gas and no active star formation. The tuning fork is not an evolutionary sequence.

Geometric distance methods use measured angles and orbital geometry. Annual parallax is the principal example. Stellar orbits near the Galactic center and orbits of maser-emitting gas clouds provide other geometric routes. Masers are microwave lasers.

A standard candle is an object whose luminosity is known. Comparing its luminosity L with its apparent brightness b gives distance through b = L / (4πd²).

Understanding checkpoint

Luminosity describes the object’s output. Apparent brightness describes the light received here.

Worked example

For two objects of the same luminosity, the fainter one is farther away. First establish the luminosity, then use the observed brightness.

Spiral nebulae and the Great Debate

Immanuel Kant proposed island universes other systems of stars beyond our own in 1755. In 1845 William Parsons, the third Earl of Rosse, built the Leviathan telescope in Ireland: 60 feet long with a 1.8 m diameter. He discovered spiral structure in some nebulae.

At the April 1920 Great Debate Shapley and Curtis disagreed about whether spiral nebulae were inside the Milky Way. Harlow Shapley placed them within our Galaxy. Heber Curtis argued that they were separate galaxies. Curtis was the stronger speaker, but the debate did not settle the evidence.

The decisive measurement was distance. Edwin Hubble, born in Missouri in 1889, first studied law at Oxford and completed his astronomy PhD in 1917. His later observations supplied the distance evidence needed to settle the question.

Understanding checkpoint

A persuasive debate could not determine the objects’ distances. Observations were needed.

Worked example

Compare the two claims: an object inside the Milky Way and a separate galaxy outside it predict very different distances.

Cepheids reveal other galaxies

In 1923 Hubble initially thought he had found a nova in Andromeda. It was a Cepheid variable a star whose pulsation period reveals its luminosity. Its faint appearance placed it very far away. Andromeda is about 2.5 million light-years away, establishing that spiral nebulae are galaxies beyond the Milky Way.

The distance sequence is identify a Cepheid → measure brightness and period → use period to find luminosity → combine luminosity and brightness to find distance.

The historical picture widened from Aristotle’s Earth-centered celestial spheres to Kapteyn’s 55,000-light-year Galaxy with the solar system at its center. Shapley moved us away from the center and proposed a 300,000-light-year Galaxy, twice too large. Hubble established a universe of many separate galaxies.

Understanding checkpoint

The Cepheid period supplies luminosity; apparent brightness then supplies the distance comparison.

Worked example

Follow the four numbered steps in the lecture’s distance diagram. The period–luminosity graph links the measured period to the luminosity used in the brightness equation.

Redshifts and Hubble’s law

In 1914 Vesto Slipher found redshifts in 11 of 15 spiral nebulae. Hubble and Humason combined redshifts with Cepheid distances in 1929, using Mount Wilson’s 100-inch (2.5 m) telescope.

Hubble’s law relates recession velocity to distance: v = H₀d. Use H₀ = 72 km/s/Mpc, v in km/s and d in Mpc. More distant galaxies recede faster. On a velocity-versus-distance plot, H₀ is the slope.

Hubble’s early nearby-galaxy measurements had inaccurate distances, but later data confirmed the relationship. The lecture’s 1000 Mpc example corresponds to about 3.3 billion light-years, described as about a quarter of the way to the observable universe’s edge.

Understanding checkpoint

To find distance, divide recession velocity by H₀. Keep km/s in the velocity so the result is in Mpc.

Worked example

The supplied example gives v = 72,000 km/s. Using d = v/H₀ gives 72,000 ÷ 72 = 1000 Mpc.

Redshift and expanding space

Redshift z measures the fractional increase in wavelength: z = Δλ/λ. For the small-redshift treatment on the slide (z < 1), z = v/c. Use c = 300,000 km/s. The lecture notes that larger redshifts require a different relativistic formula.

The meaning of Hubble’s law is that space itself is expanding. Every galaxy sees the same general expansion pattern. There is no special center. The balloon illustration shows how separations grow as its surface expands.

The lecture relates z = 0.24 to about 24% of the way to the observable universe’s edge within its small-redshift discussion.

Understanding checkpoint

A galaxy seeing other galaxies recede does not make that galaxy a special center.

Worked example

For the supplied velocity 72,000 km/s, z = 72,000 ÷ 300,000 = 0.24. The wavelength has increased by 24%.

Keep these straight

  • Standard candle → known luminosity; apparent brightness then gives distance.
  • Shapley placed spiral nebulae inside the Milky Way; Curtis argued they were separate galaxies.
  • Cepheid → identify → measure brightness and period → find luminosity → find distance.
  • Hubble’s law → v = H₀d, with H₀ = 72 km/s/Mpc.
  • Small redshifts → z = v/c. Expanding space has no special center.
